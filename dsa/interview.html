The idea

A cooking show, not a cooking exam

On a cooking show, the judges taste the dish, but they also watch the chef explain choices, adjust when something burns, and plate on time. A coding interview is the same. A perfect solution delivered in silence scores worse than a good solution where the interviewer heard your reasoning, saw you test it, and watched you recover from a mistake.

Scoring

What Google interviewers write down

Google's public hiring guidance names four areas. Coding rounds feed mostly the first two, and every round contributes to the last.

AttributeWhat it looks like in a coding round
General cognitive abilityHow you break the problem down, find the brute force, and improve it
Role-related knowledgeChoosing the right data structure, correct Big-O, clean working code
LeadershipDriving the conversation, proposing options, owning the plan
GoogleynessCollaborating with the interviewer, handling feedback well, humility

The routine

Seven steps for every coding question

A pilot runs the same takeoff checklist on every flight, even after 10,000 flights. Use the same seven steps on every practice problem until they're automatic, so stress can't knock them out.

StepMinutesWhat you do and say
1. Clarify3–5Restate the problem. Ask about input size, empty input, duplicates, negatives, sorted?, return format.
2. Examples2–3Work one normal and one edge example by hand. Confirm expected output.
3. Brute force2State it and its Big-O. "This works but is O(n²)."
4. Optimize5–10Name the bottleneck and the pattern that removes it. Get agreement before coding.
5. Code15–20Clean names, small helper functions, narrate as you go.
6. Test5Trace your example line by line. Then run an edge case. Fix bugs calmly.
7. Analyze2Final time and space complexity, and one possible improvement or trade-off.
Out loud

Phrases that show your thinking

A GPS that says "recalculating" is reassuring; one that goes silent makes you think it's broken. Narrating keeps the interviewer confident you're making progress.

  • "Before I start, can I check a few assumptions about the input?"
  • "The brute force is to try every pair, which is O(n²). The repeated work is the inner search, so a hashmap could make that O(1)."
  • "I'm choosing BFS rather than DFS because we want the fewest steps."
  • "Let me trace this with the example to make sure the pointers move correctly."
  • "I see a bug here: when the list has one node, fast.next is None. Let me guard that."
  • "This uses O(n) extra space. If memory mattered more, I could sort and use two pointers instead, at O(n log n) time."
When stuck

A ladder for getting unstuck

Lost on a hike, you don't sit down; you retrace to the last trail marker, check the map, and try the next path. Being stuck in an interview is normal. What's graded is what you do next.

Solve a tiny example by hand and watch what you do. Your hands often find the algorithm.

Go back to the brute force and ask what work is repeated.

Walk the pattern finder list in your head: sorted? contiguous? all answers? top k? graph?

Simplify: solve it for a sorted array, or with no duplicates, or for k = 1, then generalize.

Say what you're thinking and ask, "Am I on the right track?" Using a hint well is a positive signal, not a failure.

Googleyness and leadership

Behavioral answers with STAR

A good story at a dinner party has a setting, a problem, what you did, and how it ended. STAR is that, in 2 minutes: Situation, Task, Action, Result. Spend most of the time on Action, and use "I," not "we."

Example: "Tell me about a difficult bug."

Situation: "Our checkout page started timing out for about 2% of users after a release."

Task: "I was on call and needed to find the cause without rolling back a release with an important fix."

Action: "I compared slow and normal requests in the logs and saw the slow ones all had large carts. I found an N+1 query that grew with cart size, rewrote it as one batched query, and added a load test with a 200-item cart."

Result: "Timeouts dropped to zero, p95 latency improved 40%, and the load test now runs in CI so it can't regress."

Prepare 6–8 stories you can adapt:

  • A hard bug or production issue you diagnosed
  • A disagreement with a teammate or manager, and how it was resolved
  • A project with unclear or changing requirements
  • A failure or mistake, and what you changed afterwards
  • Leading or influencing without formal authority
  • Helping or mentoring someone
  • Learning a new technology quickly
  • Making something more accessible or inclusive for users
Logistics

The week before and the day of

Marathon runners don't train hardest the week before the race; they taper, rest, and lay out their kit the night before. Do the same.

  • Week before: only review your mistakes log and redo 1–2 problems per topic. No new topics.
  • Do two full mocks in a plain Google Doc, out loud, with a timer.
  • Prepare 2–3 questions to ask each interviewer about their team and work.
  • Day of: sleep, water, a working camera and mic (if virtual), a pen and paper for sketches.
  • Between rounds: let the last round go. Each interviewer scores independently.
