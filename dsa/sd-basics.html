System design

System design basics

System design is planning how a whole product works once millions of people use it: which machines, which storage, and how data moves between them. This page gives you the vocabulary and the math every later page builds on.

The idea

From a food truck to a restaurant chain

A food truck with one cook works fine for 50 customers a day. At 5,000 a day you need more cooks (more servers), a host who seats people (a load balancer), prepped ingredients by the stove (a cache), a pantry and a warehouse (databases and storage), and order tickets so cooks aren't interrupted (queues). At 5 million a day across many cities you need copies of the restaurant everywhere (data centers and CDNs) and a plan for when a kitchen catches fire (redundancy).

System design is deciding which of those pieces you need, how big they are, and how they talk to each other. Coding interviews ask "can you write the recipe?" System design interviews ask "can you run the kitchen?"

The life of a request

What happens when you open an app

Mailing a letter: you look up the address (DNS), the post office routes it to the right sorting center (load balancer), a clerk handles it (app server), and checks a quick-reference card before digging through the archive (cache, then database).

  • Client: the browser or phone app. It speaks HTTP(S) to the backend.
  • DNS: turns a name into an IP address. Results are cached for minutes to hours.
  • CDN: servers close to users that hold copies of static files (images, video, JS).
  • Load balancer: spreads requests across many servers and skips broken ones.
  • App servers: run your code. Kept stateless so any one can serve any request.
  • Cache: a fast in-memory copy of hot data (Redis, Memcached).
  • Database: the durable source of truth.
Requirements

Functional vs non-functional

Building a house: "3 bedrooms and a kitchen" is functional (what it does). "Survives an earthquake, warm in winter, built in 6 months" is non-functional (how well it does it). Two houses with identical rooms can need totally different foundations.

Functional: what users can do

  • Shorten a URL; open a short URL
  • Post a photo; see a feed
  • Send a message; see read receipts

Non-functional: how well

  • Scale: users, requests per second, data size
  • Latency: e.g. feed loads in < 200 ms
  • Availability: e.g. 99.99% uptime
  • Consistency: must every reader see the newest data?
  • Durability: never lose a message

The first 5 minutes of every system design interview is agreeing on both lists. Candidates who skip this design the wrong system confidently, and that's the most common reason for a weak score.

Vocabulary

Latency, throughput, availability

A highway. Latency is how long one car takes to drive from A to B. Throughput is how many cars pass per hour. Adding lanes raises throughput but doesn't make any single car faster. Availability is how often the highway is open.

TermMeaningTypical unit
LatencyTime for one requestms; quote p50 and p99 (the slowest 1%)

The nines

AvailabilityDowntime per yearPer month
99% (two nines)3.65 days~7.3 hours
99.9%8.8 hours~44 minutes
99.99%53 minutes~4.4 minutes
99.999%5.3 minutes~26 seconds

Parts in a chain multiply: two services at 99.9% each give 99.9% × 99.9% ≈ 99.8% together. Redundant copies in parallel do the opposite: two independent 99% servers where either one is enough give 1 − 0.01² = 99.99%.

Numbers to know

How fast things are

If reading from memory took 1 second, reading from an SSD would take about 15 minutes, and a round trip from California to Europe would take about 5 years. That's why caches and nearby servers matter so much.

OperationApprox. time
Read from CPU cache~1 ns
Read from main memory (RAM)~100 ns
Read 1 MB sequentially from RAM~10–250 µs
SSD random read~16–100 µs
Round trip inside one data center~0.5 ms
Redis/Memcached get (over network)~0.5–1 ms
Simple indexed database query~1–10 ms
Hard disk seek~10 ms
Round trip California ↔ Europe~150 ms

Exact numbers vary by hardware. Interviewers want the order of magnitude: memory ≪ SSD ≪ network across the world.

Back-of-envelope math

Estimating scale in two minutes

Planning a wedding: 200 guests × 1.5 drinks each × 4 hours tells you how many bartenders to hire. Rough numbers are enough to avoid hiring one bartender or fifty. System estimates do the same for servers and storage.

ShortcutValue

1 million requests/day≈ 12 per second (use ~10)

Worked example: a photo-sharing app

Users: 100 million daily active users (DAU).

Writes: 10% upload 1 photo/day → 10 M photos/day ÷ 10⁵ s ≈ 100 uploads/s, ~300/s at peak.

Reads: each user views 50 photos/day → 5 B views/day ≈ 50,000 views/s. Reads outnumber writes 500 : 1, so caching and a CDN are essential.

Storage: 10 M photos × 2 MB = 20 TB/day ≈ 7 PB/year. That's object storage (like Google Cloud Storage or S3), not a database.

Metadata: 10 M rows × ~1 KB = 10 GB/day, about 3.7 TB/year. One big database could hold a year, but you'll plan to shard.

Say your assumptions out loud and round aggressively (86,400 → 10⁵). The interviewer cares that your numbers lead to decisions ("reads dominate, so cache"), not that they're precise.

Mindset

Everything is a trade-off

Buying a car: fast, cheap, or reliable. You can usually pick two. Every design choice gives something up, and naming what you give up is the skill being tested.

You gainYou usually pay with
Speed (cache)Possibly stale data, more memory
Availability (replicas)Consistency, cost
Strong consistencyLatency, availability during network failures
Scale (sharding)Complexity, harder joins and transactions
Decoupling (queues)Delay before work finishes, harder debugging
