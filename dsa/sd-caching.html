System design

Caching

A cache keeps a copy of popular data somewhere faster than its source. It's the single biggest performance win in most designs, and its two hard problems are keeping copies fresh and deciding what to throw away.

The idea

The fridge, the pantry, and the supermarket

Milk is in your fridge (seconds away), rice is in the pantry (a minute), and everything else is at the supermarket (30 minutes). You keep what you use most in the closest, smallest place. A cache is your fridge: small, fast, and you have to notice when the milk goes off (stale data) and throw things out when it's full (eviction).

Cache layerWhereExample

Database cacheInside the DBBuffer pool of hot pages

Rule of thumb: about 20% of the data gets about 80% of the reads, so caching a small slice of hot data removes most of the load.

Strategies

Five caching strategies

1. Cache-aside (lazy loading)

Most common

A receptionist who doesn't know a phone number checks the big directory, then writes it on a sticky note by the phone. Next time it's on the note. Nobody writes notes in advance for numbers no one asks for.

Howthe app checks the cache; on a miss it reads the database and fills the cache. Writes go to the database and delete the cache entry.

def get_user(uid):
    user = cache.get(f"user:{uid}")
    if user is None:                       # miss
        user = db.query("SELECT * FROM users WHERE id=%s", uid)
        cache.set(f"user:{uid}", user, ttl=3600)
    return user

def update_user(uid, data):
    db.update(uid, data)
    cache.delete(f"user:{uid}")               # next read refills it

Pros: only caches what's used; a cache crash just means slower reads. Cons: the first read is slow; data can be stale until TTL or delete.

2. Write-through

Cache always fresh

A shop that updates the price tag on the shelf and the price in the computer at the same moment. Both always agree, but every price change takes a little longer.

Howevery write goes to the cache and the database together. Reads are always fresh. Writes are slower, and you may cache data nobody reads.

3. Write-back (write-behind)

Fastest writes, risk of loss

Writing notes on a whiteboard during a meeting and typing them up later. Very fast in the moment, but if someone wipes the board before you type them, they're gone.

Howwrites go to the cache, which flushes to the database later in batches. Great for counters like view counts and likes; risky for money or anything you can't lose.

4. Write-around

Don't cache what won't be reread

Filing old tax records straight into the archive box rather than on your desk, because you probably won't look at them again soon.

Howwrites skip the cache and go straight to the database; the cache fills only on reads. Good for logs, uploads and data written once and rarely read.

5. Read-through

Cache does the loading

A library assistant who fetches a book from storage when you ask at the desk. You only ever talk to the desk; you never go to storage yourself.

Howlike cache-aside, but the cache library loads from the database on a miss, so app code only talks to the cache.

Eviction

What to throw out when the cache is full

A full closet. You could toss whatever you haven't worn longest (LRU), whatever you wear least often (LFU), or the oldest thing you bought (FIFO). Most people use LRU without thinking about it.

PolicyEvictsGood for
LRU (least recently used)Item untouched for longestMost workloads; the default
LFU (least frequently used)Item used least oftenStable popularity (top songs)
FIFOOldest insertedSimple, rarely the best
TTL (time to live)Anything past its expiryBounding staleness; combine with LRU

An LRU cache is the classic coding question too: a hashmap for O(1) lookup plus a doubly linked list for O(1) "move to front" and "remove oldest." See Linked lists.

Hard parts

Invalidation, stampedes, and hot keys

A famous joke says there are two hard things in computer science: cache invalidation and naming things. Like a printed train timetable, a cached copy is useful until the schedule changes and nobody reprints it.

Stale data. Use a TTL as a safety net, and delete (not update) the cache entry on writes. Deleting avoids a race where two writers leave the older value in the cache.

Cache stampede (thundering herd). A popular key expires and 10,000 requests all miss at once and hammer the database. Fixes: a lock so only one request refills while others wait or get the old value; refresh before expiry; add random jitter to TTLs so keys don't all expire together.

Hot keys. One key (a celebrity's profile, the World Cup final score) gets so many reads that its single cache node melts. Fixes: replicate that key to several nodes, add a tiny in-process cache on each app server, or split it into key#1…key#10.

Cold start. After a restart the cache is empty and everything misses. Warm it with known-popular keys, or bring nodes back gradually.

Cache penetration. Requests for keys that don't exist always miss and hit the database. Cache the "not found" result briefly, or use a Bloom filter.

Deep analysis

How much does a cache help?

If 9 out of 10 shopping trips are just to the fridge, your average trip is about 3 minutes instead of 30, even though the supermarket didn't get any closer.

Average latency = hit rate × cache time + miss rate × (cache time + database time).

Hit rateAvg latency (cache 1 ms, DB 20 ms)DB queries/s for 50,000 reads/s
0%20 ms (no cache)50,000
80%0.8 + 0.2 × 21 = 5.0 ms10,000
95%0.95 + 0.05 × 21 = 2.0 ms2,500
99%0.99 + 0.01 × 21 = 1.2 ms500

Going from 95% to 99% hits cuts database load 5×. That's why teams fight for every point of hit rate.

When you add a cache in an interview, say four things: what's cached and its key, the strategy (usually cache-aside), the TTL, and how writes invalidate it. Then mention stampede protection. That's a complete answer.
