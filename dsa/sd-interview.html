System design

The system design interview

A 45-minute conversation where you design a system out loud. There's no single right answer; you're scored on structure, trade-offs, and communication. This page is the routine to rehearse, a cheat sheet to review, and a study plan.

The idea

An architect meeting a client

A client tells an architect "I want a house." A bad architect starts drawing a mansion immediately. A good one asks: how many people, what budget, which climate, do you work from home? Then sketches the floor plan, explains why the kitchen faces the garden, and adjusts when the client says "actually, we need a garage." System design interviews reward the second architect.

For Software Engineer III (L4): Google's loop for this level is mostly coding, but a system design round can be included, and coding rounds often end with design-flavored follow-ups ("how would this work at a billion users?"). Ask your recruiter exactly which rounds you'll have. Either way, L4 is expected to produce a sensible, working design and discuss trade-offs; deep expertise in distributed consensus is a senior-level expectation.

The routine

A 45-minute framework

A cooking competition with a clock: prep, cook, plate, present. If you spend 40 minutes on the sauce, there's no dish to taste. Watch the time and move on.

StepMinutesWhat you do
1. Requirements5Clarify features (pick 3–4 core ones), users, scale, latency, consistency needs. Write them down.
2. Estimates3–5QPS for reads and writes, storage per year, bandwidth. Note what dominates.
3. API3–54–6 endpoints with inputs and outputs.
4. Data model3–5Main entities, keys, which database and why.
5. High-level design10Boxes and arrows for the main flows. Walk one request end to end.
6. Deep dives10–15The 1–2 hardest parts: scaling the bottleneck, a tricky algorithm, consistency. Let the interviewer steer.
7. Wrap-up2–3Bottlenecks, failure handling, monitoring, what you'd do next.

Questions to ask in step 1

  • Who are the users and how many (DAU)? Which features matter most?
  • Read-heavy or write-heavy? What's the read/write ratio?
  • How fresh must data be? Is losing data ever acceptable?
  • Latency target? Global or one region? Mobile, web, or both?
Scoring

What interviewers look for

A driving test examiner doesn't only check that you reached the destination. They watch whether you checked mirrors, signaled, and explained what you'd do at a closed road.

SignalStrongWeak
ScopingAsks questions, writes requirements, picks a focusStarts drawing immediately

Trade-offs"I'd choose X because…, at the cost of…"Lists technologies without reasons

CommunicationThinks aloud, checks in, adapts to hintsSilent, defensive about feedback

Out loud

Phrases that show good judgment

A tour guide narrates as they walk: "on your left… we're heading here because…" Narration keeps the group with you and shows you know where you're going.

  • "Let me confirm scope: I'll focus on posting and viewing the feed, and leave out search and ads unless you'd like them."
  • "Reads outnumber writes about 100 to 1, so I'll optimize the read path first with a cache."
  • "I'll start with one SQL database for simplicity and shard by user_id once we pass a few terabytes."
  • "This is eventually consistent: a new post may take a couple of seconds to appear for followers, which fits the requirement."
  • "The bottleneck here is the fan-out for celebrities. Two options are… I'd pick the hybrid because…"
  • "If this service fails, we degrade gracefully: the feed still loads, without recommendations."
Avoid these

Common mistakes

Building a skyscraper's penthouse before pouring the foundation looks impressive for a minute and then falls over.

  • Skipping requirements and designing the wrong thing.
  • Naming technologies ("Kafka, Cassandra, Kubernetes") without saying why.
  • Going deep on one detail before an end-to-end design exists.
  • Over-engineering a small system (microservices and sharding for 1,000 users).
  • Ignoring failure: no replication, no timeouts, single points of failure.
  • Never doing math, or doing math and never using the result.
  • Not listening: missing the interviewer's hint about what they want to explore.
Study plan

Four weeks, alongside your DSA plan

Learning a language: vocabulary first, then sentences, then real conversations. Don't jump to mock interviews before the building blocks feel familiar.
